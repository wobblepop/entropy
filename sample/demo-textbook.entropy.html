Introduction

Intro3 minoverview

An overview of supply chain risk and why it matters in the modern world.

Supply chain risk encompasses the potential for disruptions, failures, and vulnerabilities across the network of organizations, people, activities, information, and resources involved in moving a product or service from supplier to customer.

This textbook explores risk from multiple angles — structural, behavioral, and systemic — giving you the tools to identify, assess, and mitigate threats before they cascade.

Foundations of Supply Chain Management

Intro8 minfundamentals

Core concepts and terminology you'll need throughout this textbook.

A supply chain is not a chain at all — it's a network. Understanding this distinction is the first step toward understanding where risk hides.

Key Concepts

  • Nodes: Organizations or facilities in the network
  • Links: Relationships and flows between nodes
  • Tiers: Layers of suppliers (Tier 1 = direct, Tier 2 = supplier's supplier, etc.)
  • Visibility: How far into the network you can see

The further from your direct operations, the less visibility you typically have — and the more risk accumulates undetected.

The Visibility Problem

Intermediate7 minvisibilitycomplexity

Why organizations can't see beyond Tier 1, and what that blindness costs them.

Most organizations have reasonable visibility into their Tier 1 suppliers. Beyond that? Darkness.

A 2023 study found that only 6% of companies had full visibility into their Tier 3 suppliers. This isn't negligence — it's structural. Each tier multiplies complexity geometrically.

Why It Matters

The 2011 Thai floods, the 2021 Suez Canal blockage, semiconductor shortages — all exploited visibility gaps. Companies discovered critical dependencies only after disruption.

Network Mapping Techniques

Advanced20 minmappingmethodologytools

Methods for mapping your supply network beyond direct relationships.

Mapping a supply network is like mapping a cave system — you proceed methodically, document as you go, and accept that complete maps may be impossible.

Approaches

  1. Survey-based: Ask suppliers about their suppliers (low cost, low reliability)
  2. Data-driven: Use shipping records, customs data, financial flows
  3. Technology-enabled: Blockchain traceability, IoT sensors, satellite monitoring
  4. Hybrid: Combine methods, triangulate

No single method is sufficient. The most robust maps layer multiple data sources.

Categories of Supply Chain Risk

Intermediate12 mintaxonomyrisk

A taxonomy of risks: operational, financial, reputational, strategic, and compliance.

Risk in supply chains doesn't come in one flavor. A useful taxonomy:

  1. Operational Risk — disruptions to flow (disasters, capacity failures, quality issues)
  2. Financial Risk — currency fluctuation, supplier insolvency, price volatility
  3. Reputational Risk — association with unethical practices, recalls, scandals
  4. Strategic Risk — dependency on single sources, intellectual property exposure
  5. Compliance Risk — regulatory violations, sanctions, environmental law

Each category requires different detection methods and different mitigation strategies.

Operational Risk in Depth

Intermediate10 minoperationaldisruptionmitigation

Disruptions, quality failures, and capacity constraints — the risks that stop the flow.

Operational risk is the most tangible category — when it materializes, product stops moving.

Sources

  • Natural disasters (earthquakes, floods, pandemics)
  • Infrastructure failures (port closures, power outages)
  • Quality escapes (defective components reaching production)
  • Capacity mismatches (demand spikes, supplier constraints)

Mitigation Strategies

  • Dual/multi-sourcing for critical components
  • Safety stock buffers (cost vs. risk tradeoff)
  • Geographic diversification
  • Supplier financial health monitoring

Financial Risk in Supply Chains

Intermediate8 minfinancialinsolvencymonitoring

When money flows go wrong: insolvency, currency, and price volatility.

Financial risk in supply chains often arrives without warning. A key supplier's bankruptcy can halt production overnight.

Warning Signs

  • Delayed payments to their own suppliers
  • Sudden changes in payment terms requested
  • Loss of key customers
  • Leadership turnover
  • Credit rating downgrades

Protective Measures

  • Regular financial health assessments
  • Diversified supplier base
  • Contractual protections (step-in rights, escrow)
  • Supply chain finance programs to support supplier liquidity

Human Behavior and Deception

Intermediate15 minbehaviorfraudpsychology

How cognitive biases, trust, and deliberate deception create hidden vulnerabilities.

The most sophisticated risk models fail when they ignore the human element. People don't behave rationally, and some behave deceptively.

Cognitive Biases in Risk Assessment

  • Optimism bias: Underestimating probability of negative events
  • Anchoring: Over-relying on first piece of information received
  • Status quo bias: Preferring current suppliers even when evidence suggests switching

Deliberate Deception

Fraud in supply chains is not rare — it's endemic. From counterfeit components to falsified certifications, deception exploits the trust gaps inherent in complex networks.

A Typology of Supply Chain Fraud

Advanced15 minfraudtypologydetection

Categorizing deception: counterfeiting, diversion, document fraud, and more.

Supply chain fraud takes many forms, each exploiting different trust mechanisms:

Categories

  1. Product fraud: Counterfeit goods, adulteration, substitution
  2. Document fraud: Falsified certificates, forged inspections, fake origins
  3. Relationship fraud: Shell companies, kickbacks, undisclosed conflicts
  4. Process fraud: Bypassed quality checks, falsified test results
  5. Financial fraud: Invoice manipulation, phantom shipments

Detection Challenges

Fraud is designed to be invisible. Unlike accidental failures, fraudsters actively adapt to detection methods. This creates an arms race between oversight and evasion.

Case Study: The Counterfeit Components Crisis

Intermediate12 mincase-studycounterfeitdefenseelectronics

How fake electronic parts infiltrated defense supply chains and what it revealed.

In 2011, a U.S. Senate investigation revealed that over one million counterfeit electronic parts had entered the defense supply chain. The parts — many sourced from e-waste recycling operations in China — were relabeled and sold as new through layers of brokers.

What Went Wrong

  • Procurement prioritized cost over provenance
  • Multi-tier supply chains obscured origins
  • Testing was insufficient to detect sophisticated counterfeits
  • Trust was given based on paperwork, not verification

Lessons

  1. Document-based trust is exploitable
  2. Cost pressure creates fraud incentives
  3. Visibility gaps are fraud opportunities
  4. Detection requires investment proportional to risk

Discussion Questions

  • At which tier did visibility fail?
  • What cognitive biases contributed to the failure to detect?
  • How would you redesign the procurement process?
